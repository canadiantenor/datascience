Kitchen table D&D · adventure one

The Bell of Barleybrook

Three fights, one puzzle, one pile of treasure, and a dragon who only wanted to learn how to sing. Written for a child of about six to eight who has never played before, and a grown-up who maybe hasn't either.

Ages 6–81–3 kids plus a Dungeon Master60–75 minutesLevel 1Continues in The Mother of Copperfoot
The whole game

The rules, in one minute

Read this out loud. There is nothing else to learn.

You say, then you roll

Say what your hero does. If it's easy it just happens. If it's hard or risky, roll the d20, add your number, beat 10.

Attacking

d20 + your attack number against the monster's AC. Equal or higher is a hit, for 1 damage — fill in one circle.

Natural 20

The die itself shows 20: a critical hit. Roll a d6 and do that much damage instead.

Three hearts each

Fill your own circles when you're hit. All full and you fall over asleep. Nobody dies. You wake with 1 hit point when the fight ends.

Advantage

Roll two d20s and keep the better one. Hand it out freely for any clever idea. Disadvantage is the same, keeping the worse.

Turn order

Monsters first, then the heroes in whatever order they like. No rolling for who goes when.

The one rule for youA monster always goes for the hero with the most hit points left, and never attacks the same hero twice in a row. That quietly keeps the 4-hit-point wizard alive without you having to fudge anything.
No d20 in the house?Roll three ordinary six-sided dice and use the small grey number printed on every card instead. That swap comes from Wizards of the Coast's own kids' adventure.
Loot

Treasure you choose

Barleybrook has its own 23-card deck — apples, chalk, a borrowed teapot, one saved firework. After every fight, deal three Common cards face up and let them keep one. The argument about which to take is the reward. The Glitter Hall in room 5 is the one chest: deal 3 Rare there. Relics carry forward into adventure two's deck.

RelicsClink hands over a relic at the end. Relics are never spent — lay it face up in front of the player and leave it there between sessions. Next adventure begins with it already on the table.

When can you use one?

Every card tells you, in the bottom right corner.

On your turn

Using it is your turn, instead of attacking. Eating something, throwing something, calling for help.

Free

Costs nothing at all. Use it any time — in the middle of somebody else's turn, or after you have already seen the die land.

Always on

Never spent. It simply keeps being true, this fight and every fight afterwards. Leave it face up on the table.

The limit: one card on your turn, and one Free card each round. Without that, a good hand turns into a five-minute monologue and the fight stops being a fight.

Deal three, keep one

Try it now

This is Barleybrook's own deck — 23 cards, different from the Copperfoot one. Tap a button, then tap the card you want.

The route

The Old Copperfoot Mine

Six rooms in a line, so nobody can get lost. Short on time? Skip room 3 or room 5 — the adventure still works.

How it starts

Read aloud

Every autumn, the village of Barleybrook rings the Harvest Bell. It is enormous, it is older than anybody's grandmother, and when it rings you can hear it in three valleys at once. Tomorrow is the Harvest Festival.

This morning the bell is gone. There is a bell-shaped hole in the wet grass where it stood, and a trail of scratches leading up the hill — toward the old copper mine that nobody has gone into for a hundred years.

The baker looks at you. The blacksmith looks at you. Everybody looks at you, because you are the only ones brave enough to be standing this close to the hill.

Then ask"What's the first thing your hero does?" Whatever they say, say yes to it. Then go up the hill.
Room one

The Mine Mouth

5 min · no fight
Read aloud

The mine mouth is a black hole in the hillside, propped open with old wooden beams. A sign hangs crooked beside it. Something small is sitting on top of the sign holding a lantern, and it is a mouse wearing a tiny hat.

"Oh good," says the mouse. "Help. I'm Tam. Something very large went past me last night and took my sign's best bell, and also the enormous one from the village. I would go after it myself, but look at me."

  • Tam hands over the Everbright Lantern if anybody asks for light, and comes along. He is how you nudge them later without taking over — a hint can always be something Tam mutters.
  • The sign is the puzzle clue. Read it aloud twice, slowly, then write the three capitalised words on a scrap of paper and hand it over. They need it in room 4, and carrying it the whole way is most of the satisfaction.
The sign says

Three bells hang in Copperfoot deep.
Ring them wrong and the rocks will weep.
First the SMALL one, bright and thin,
then the CRACKED one — let it ring,
then the BIG one, low and slow,
and the old stone door will go.

Room two

The Cart Room

15 min · fight one
Read aloud

The tunnel opens into a round room full of broken mine carts. Three small green goblins are sitting in the biggest one, going through a sack of stolen things. One of them is wearing a teapot as a hat. They have not noticed you yet.

  • Give them a free moment first. "They haven't seen you. What do you want to do?" Anything clever — sneaking up, rolling a cart at them, shouting BOO — earns advantage on the first attack. This is the moment that teaches them thinking beats rolling.
  • This fight is the tutorial. Go slowly and say the numbers out loud: "Twelve? Their AC is ten, so you hit. Fill in a circle." Goblins have 2 hit points, so a critical usually drops one in a single swing — a very good thing to happen in somebody's first fight.
  • End it early. The moment one goblin is left it drops the sack and bolts.
In the sack12 gold pieces, a very loud whistle, and a dried sausage about the size of a boot. Make sure somebody picks the sausage up. Do not explain why.
RewardDeal 3 Common treasure cards. They keep one.
Room three

Chompy's Pool

12 min · fight two, or not
Read aloud

The tunnel widens around a still green pool. Lying across the only path, with its tail in the water, is a lizard the size of a pony. Its eyes are milky white and its nose is twitching. Every time it breathes out, bubbles come up through the water.

  • Three ways past, all correct. Throw the sausage — no roll, Chompy lumbers off to eat it. Sneak — everybody rolls d20 against 10, with advantage for a good idea. Fight — he is slow, half blind, and has 6 hit points.
  • If they get past without fighting, say so out loud: "You beat that one without a single swing. That counts." Seven-year-olds need telling once, and then they start looking for it every time.
  • If the fight goes badly, Chompy stops at 3 circles, backs off hissing, and lets them past. He was guarding his pool, not hunting them.
RewardDeal 3 Common cards — whether they fought him or fed him.
Room four

The Three Bells

12 min · the puzzle
Read aloud

A huge stone door blocks the tunnel, with no handle and no keyhole. Set into the rock beside it are three bells on three chains: a small bright one, a big dusty one as wide as a cartwheel, and a cracked one with a split down its side. Above them, scratched into the stone: RING US RIGHT.

  • The answer is small, cracked, big — the order from Tam's sign in room 1.
  • A wrong bell makes the rocks weep: a shower of pebbles, 1 damage to the hero with the most hit points, and the bells reset. They can try as often as they like. Nobody gets locked out of their own adventure.
  • Let them struggle a few minutes, then use the hints. The goal is that they solve it, not that it was hard.
Hint one

"Do you still have that bit of paper?" Read the rhyme again, slowly, leaning on the three capital words.

Hint two

Tam hums it: "Small… cracked… big. That's the tune of it, I think."

Hint three

Tam runs over and taps the small bell with his tail. "This one first. I'm almost sure."

If they invent something better

Breaking the door, squeezing through a crack, sending Tam under it — let it work, and give them the treasure anyway.

Room five

The Glitter Hall

8 min · the chest
Read aloud

The door grinds open onto a cave that is full of noise-making things. Cowbells. Wind chimes. A cracked trumpet. Hundreds of little silver bells, all sorted into neat piles by size. And gold — actual gold, in actual heaps.

On the wall, scratched into the rock with a claw, is a drawing: a small dragon with its mouth open, standing in front of a crowd of people. The people are all smiling. It has been drawn over and over, hundreds of times.

  • Let them take everything. Don't ration it. 40 gold pieces — ten each — and counting it out loud is half the scene.
  • The drawing is the whole story and it pays off in two minutes. Describe it, then say nothing else about it. If they ask Tam, Tam has no idea.
  • There is a wind-up music box in the pile that plays one short, sad little tune. Mention it once, in passing.
ChestDeal 3 Rare cards face up. They keep one. Let them hold all three first.
Room six

The Singing Cave

15 min · the boss
Read aloud

The last cave is enormous, and the Harvest Bell is standing right in the middle of it, as tall as a door. Curled around it is a dragon — a young one, copper-coloured, about the size of a big dog. It is holding a tiny hammer.

It hits the bell. BONNNNG. The sound rolls around the cave. The dragon opens its mouth and tries to make the same sound, and what comes out is a squeak.

Then it sees you, goes very still, and says: "It's mine. Go away."

  • Two ways to win, both real. Fight him, or reach him.
  • Fighting: 10 hit points. At the fifth circle he stops on his own, sits down in the middle of the floor and cries, and the bell rolls out of his claws. That is the safety valve — the fight cannot run away from you.
  • Reaching him: singing to him, showing him the drawings, offering the music box, or simply asking why. Roll d20 against 10, with advantage if they use the drawing or the box. If a kid thinks of this on their own, skip the roll and let it just work.
  • Either way, end here: Clink can't sing. He took the bell because it makes the most beautiful sound he has ever heard and he wanted to learn how. He'll give it back if somebody promises to teach him.
Read aloud at the end

The Harvest Festival happens the next evening, and the Harvest Bell rings out over three valleys, exactly on time.

And standing on the roof of the bell tower, slightly out of tune but extremely loud, a small copper dragon is singing along. He is getting better. Everybody claps.

Boss relicClink gives them a copper scale from the bell's clapper. Deal a Relic card. It is never used up.

When it goes sideways

He attacks Tam

Tam dodges — he's very small and very fast — and is hurt, loudly and theatrically. Most kids apologise. Don't punish it beyond that.

He's frightened

Turn the lights up and make the monster sillier. The goblin is wearing a teapot. Chompy burps. Fear leaves a scene fast when something is ridiculous.

He's lost interest

Jump to the next fight or hand over the treasure. Rooms 3 and 5 are both skippable. Finishing early beats fizzling out.

He wants to do something not on the sheet

Say yes. Ask "how does that work?", then ask for a d20 against 10. The best habit you can build in session one.

Every hero is knocked out

They wake outside the mine with Tam standing over them looking worried, everything they collected still in the bag. Go back in. There is no losing.

He wants to be the Dungeon Master

Let him, next time. Hand him a monster card tonight and let him roll its attacks against you.

After the adventure

  • Level up. Every hero gains +2 hit points — draw two more circles — and +1 to their attack number. Let them write it in themselves.
  • Hand out a title. Bell-Finder. Sausage-Thrower. Dragon's Singing Teacher. Write it on the sheet and keep the sheets in a folder; that folder is now a campaign.
  • Next time, add one real rule. One is plenty per session: a grid and minis, rolling for who goes first, or letting them build a character from scratch.
  • Clink is the hook for adventure two. Dragons have parents. His is going to come looking for him, and she is not the size of a big dog.
Credit where it's due

Where this came from

The simplified rules aren't invented — they're lifted from people who already solved this problem.

  • Monster Slayers: The Champions of the Elements (Wizards of the Coast, free, ages 6+). The backbone: d20 + bonus against AC, one point of damage per hit, hit points as circles you fill in, the 3d6 alternative, the rule that monsters go for the healthiest hero, and the candy-per-damage trick. Its predecessor, The Heroes of Hesiod, is also free.
  • Tenkar's Tavern on Heroes of Hesiod — the criticism that the official version is all combat and no roleplaying is fair, and it's exactly why this one has a mouse, a puzzle, a treasure room and a boss you can talk to.
  • Hero Kids by Justin Halliday (ages 4–10) — where the no-reading-required idea comes from. Worth buying if this lands well.
  • Running a D&D Game for First-Time Players and Games for Kids: DMing Advice — pregenerated characters, sessions under an hour, plunge into the action, explain a rule only when it comes up.
  • D&D Adventure Club — G-rated modules written for kids by a family on Bainbridge Island, run by libraries for ages 8–15. The natural next purchase.
  • D&D Basic Rules — free and official, for when he starts asking what else his character can do.

Barleybrook, Tam, Chompy and Clink are made up for this adventure. Everything mechanical is a simplification of published D&D rules, credited above.
